Today, I’m going to look at a change that now seems entirely ordinary: the decision that clocks in different places should show the same time. Railways played a central role in this, but first we need to understand the system they replaced.
Before railway travel, there was no single time used throughout Britain. Each town worked out its local time from the sun, usually treating the moment when it reached its highest point as noon. Because that moment occurs slightly later as you move west, a clock in Bristol could be several minutes behind one in London.
For most people, these differences created little difficulty. A long trip was made on foot or by horse-drawn coach, and passengers covered comparatively short distances each day. A journey between distant towns could continue into the following day, requiring an overnight stay, so a difference of five or ten minutes was insignificant. Travellers who owned watches could simply adjust them when they arrived.
The expansion of the railways altered the situation. Trains made journeys much faster, while a growing network meant that accurate timetables were essential. If a station clock followed local time while the railway used another city’s time, a passenger could arrive at what appeared to be the correct moment and still miss the train.
There was also a safety issue. Early lines often had trains travelling in both directions on a single track, with passing places at particular points. If neighbouring stations were working to different times, two trains might enter the same section, resulting in collisions rather than simply delays for passengers.
At first, individual companies imposed one time across their own routes, and the Great Western Railway was an early example. Its timetable of 1841 warned passengers that every station kept London time, and listed how many minutes this differed from clocks farther west. This reduced confusion within that railway, but didn’t provide one system for the whole country.

So where could a common time come from? The obvious institution was the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, where astronomers used precise observations of the sky to maintain accurate clocks. This work wasn’t intended to organise railways. Its practical value was clearest at sea, where sailors compared the time on board with a known reference time. This helped them calculate their position east or west and was therefore fundamental to navigation.
Railway managers gradually recognised that the observatory’s time could serve a new purpose on land. In 1847, the Railway Clearing House, which helped companies coordinate their operations, recommended that all stations adopt Greenwich time.
Adopting it in principle was easier than distributing it accurately. A stationmaster could set a clock in London, but even a reliable clock would gradually gain or lose seconds. The electric telegraph provided the solution and was already carrying written messages over long distances. From 1852, however, Greenwich began sending time signals through telegraph wires. Because distant stations received the same electrical information, their clocks could be synchronised, so they all showed the same time.
The railways had now created a common system, but public acceptance was more gradual. A station clock might show Greenwich time while a nearby town clock continued to show local time. Some clockmakers responded with timepieces that had two minute hands: one indicated local time and the other railway time.
Reactions were mixed. Many people welcomed easier planning, more reliable connections and greater agreement between public clocks. But some communities believed that railway companies were interfering in something that had always been decided locally. They protested against what was called “railway-time aggression”: human activity was being forced to follow the clock rather than the sun.
Nevertheless, Greenwich time continued to spread through stations, post offices, churches and town halls, so daily practice changed before Parliament acted. It was only in 1880 that legislation made Greenwich Mean Time Britain’s legal time. By then, railways had done more than accelerate travel: they had changed how an entire society organised the day.
