LECTURER: Today I’d like to look at how traditional buildings in hot regions remained comfortable before mechanical air conditioning. Their cooling depended on several features working together: the position of the building, the form of the surrounding settlement, and the behaviour of air, water and construction materials.

Let’s begin with limiting heat from outside. Where there was a choice, the building’s orientation was carefully considered. The path of the sun mattered, particularly the strongest afternoon sunlight. But builders also took account of the prevailing winds, turning openings towards them to admit air, or away from dust-laden air.

The settlement layout could be just as important. Narrow streets exposed less of the walls to direct sunlight. They could also channel a breeze, although not reliably in every location. More consistently, the streets reduced solar exposure, while projecting upper floors kept the buildings below in shade for much of the day.

Colour played a role too. Dark walls absorb much of the sunlight falling on them. Pale external surfaces instead reflected incoming solar radiation before it could warm the structure.

Now let’s turn to systems using air and water. Courtyard houses were arranged around an open central space. During the day, the courtyard was partly shielded by the surrounding walls, which also stored some of the heat they received. So the courtyard provided more than light and a place for domestic activity: its thermal role altered as day gave way to night.

After sunset, the stored heat began to escape. It is sometimes said that warm surfaces simply transferred this energy to the cooler night air. Some transfer occurred in that way, but it was not the whole explanation. Under clear conditions, the surfaces also released heat by radiation towards the open sky. With little cloud to return that energy, their temperature could fall quite rapidly.

The air touching these cooler surfaces became cooler and denser. Instead of rising towards the upper storeys, it collected near ground level. Low openings then allowed it to move from the courtyard into the surrounding rooms, while warmer air escaped through openings higher up.
