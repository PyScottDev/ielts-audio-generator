LECTURER: Courtyards often contained plants and sometimes a pool or fountain. These were not merely decorative. As water evaporated from leaves or the pool’s surface, it absorbed energy and provided additional cooling, particularly in dry air.

A different method can be seen in wind towers. At street level, airflow was interrupted by walls and other obstacles. The tower inlet stood above neighbouring rooftops, where moving air was stronger and steadier. Openings at the top collected this air and directed it down a vertical shaft. In simple towers, it entered the rooms directly. In other designs, the air passed across a pool or a damp surface before entering the building, so evaporation reduced its temperature further.

Finally, we need to consider materials and internal design. Thick walls of stone or earth absorbed heat during the day. This did not mean heat never passed through them. Instead, the inner surface remained cool for a long period. More precisely, the thermal mass produced a long delay before the highest outdoor temperature affected the interior. By then, the outside air might already be cooling.

This explains how the wall helped control temperature, but some earthen materials offered another benefit. They absorbed water vapour when the air was damp and released it in drier conditions, helping to control indoor humidity.

Temperature and moisture were not the only concerns. Domestic design also had to balance the movement of air with the occupants’ social needs. Privacy was especially important where windows faced a public street. Lattice screens broke up the view from outside, so passers-by could not see clearly into the rooms. Yet their many small openings did not seal the window, and air could still pass through.

At the top of the house, builders deliberately separated the ceiling from what lay above. They left a ventilated space above the ceiling and immediately beneath the outer roof. Air moving through this space carried away accumulated heat and prevented much of it from reaching the rooms below.

So these buildings combined shade, controlled airflow, evaporation and carefully chosen materials. Their effectiveness came from treating the building and its surroundings as one connected system.
