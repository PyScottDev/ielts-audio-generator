KIM: Six members told me what had helped them most when they joined Greenway.
Hana asked whether the club lent helmets and other equipment, though she already had everything essential. A leader moved her saddle slightly, but Hana’s real worry was being left behind. On her first ride, she found several other beginners who climbed hills as slowly as she did. Realising she wouldn’t struggle to keep up gave her the confidence to return.
Peter’s bicycle fitted him well, and he knew several quiet local roads. Yet he never went far because he imagined getting a puncture and being unable to continue. At a workshop he learnt to remove a wheel, replace an inner tube and put everything back correctly. Once he could solve that problem himself, longer rides no longer seemed risky.
Aisha hoped eventually to cycle to work, so a member explained where she could leave her bicycle. That became useful later. Initially, even short leisure rides seemed to require a busy main road, and she found the traffic frightening. A leader took her through side streets to a traffic-free path she hadn’t known existed. Soon she was cycling regularly without facing the road she had avoided.
Tom had registered for a charity ride, and the club had equipment to lend, although he owned what he needed. But he kept postponing training: if Sunday was wet, he promised to ride on Monday, then found another excuse. The club’s beginner ride left at nine every Sunday, so there was no decision to make. That fixed commitment, rather than the distant event alone, got him cycling consistently.
Mei’s knees and shoulders hurt whenever she cycled. She kept pace with the beginners and enjoyed a brake-repair demonstration, but neither addressed the pain. A coach lowered her saddle and changed the angle of the handlebars. On her next ride the discomfort had almost disappeared, so she stopped assuming cycling simply wasn’t suitable for her.
Daniel had cycled occasionally for years and didn’t need help with routes or repairs. Work kept him from Wednesday rides, and commuting advice was irrelevant because he worked from home. What changed his attitude was committing to an 80-kilometre fundraising ride for a local hospice. Training to complete that distance gave purpose to rides that had previously felt aimless.
Start small, identify your obstacle, and choose the support that addresses it. We hope you’ll join us.
