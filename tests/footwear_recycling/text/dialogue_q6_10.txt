NINA: We still need one problem for each proposed use. Shall we start with rubber flooring?
ALEX: I assumed the market would be small, but it’s used in gyms, playgrounds and public buildings. Transport to one plant has an environmental cost, but the article stresses the preparation: fabric, foam and metal must be removed before the rubber can be processed.
NINA: So separation is the main obstacle. For insulation panels, I wondered about the varying density of the foam.
ALEX: The manufacturer can blend different batches, so variation is manageable. The problem is supply. Some months there is plenty of foam; in others, there isn’t enough to keep production going.
NINA: New soles sound easier because the components have already been separated.
ALEX: But the recovered rubber isn’t uniform. One batch may be soft and flexible, while another contains harder material, so the manufacturer can’t guarantee the same strength and grip every time.
NINA: What about recycling the new shoes again?
ALEX: The researcher says that’s a weakness of shoe design generally. The immediate problem here is achieving consistent performance.
NINA: Bags and belts require little machinery, so energy use isn’t the issue. They can also sell for high prices.
ALEX: Yes, but only a few specialist shops stock them. Even successful designers could use only a tiny fraction of the rejected footwear.
NINA: Finally, construction blocks. There should be enough raw material, and small production units could reduce transport.
ALEX: The difficulty is manufacturing them. The mixture has to be heated and kept under pressure for a long time, so the energy requirement is much higher than it first appears.
NINA: Can the blocks be recycled afterwards?
ALEX: Apparently damaged blocks can be reheated and remoulded. We’ll identify energy use as the main concern, then. That gives us five distinct limitations for the final slide.
