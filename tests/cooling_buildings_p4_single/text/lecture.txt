Today I’d like to look at how traditional buildings in hot regions remained comfortable before mechanical air conditioning. Their cooling depended on several features working together: the position of the building, the form of the surrounding settlement, and the behaviour of air, water and construction materials.
Let’s begin with limiting heat from outside. Where there was a choice, the building’s orientation was carefully considered. The path of the sun mattered, particularly the strongest afternoon sunlight. But builders also took account of the prevailing winds, turning openings towards them to admit air, or away from dust-laden air.
The settlement layout could be just as important. Narrow streets exposed less of the walls to direct sunlight. They could also channel a breeze, although not reliably in every location. More consistently, the streets reduced solar exposure, while projecting upper floors kept the buildings below in shade for much of the day.
Colour played a role too. Dark walls absorb much of the sunlight falling on them. Pale external surfaces instead reflected incoming solar radiation before it could warm the structure.
Now let’s turn to systems using air and water. Courtyard houses were arranged around an open central space. During the day, the courtyard was partly shielded by the surrounding walls, which also stored some of the heat they received. So the courtyard provided more than light and a place for domestic activity: its thermal role altered as day gave way to night.
After sunset, the stored heat began to escape. It is sometimes said that warm surfaces simply transferred this energy to the cooler night air. Some transfer occurred in that way, but it was not the whole explanation. Under clear conditions, the surfaces also released heat by radiation towards the open sky. With little cloud to return that energy, their temperature could fall quite rapidly.
The air touching these cooler surfaces became cooler and denser. Instead of rising towards the upper storeys, it collected near ground level. Low openings then allowed it to move from the courtyard into the surrounding rooms, while warmer air escaped through openings higher up.
Courtyards often contained plants and sometimes a pool or fountain. These were not merely decorative. As water evaporated from leaves or the pool’s surface, it absorbed energy and provided additional cooling, particularly in dry air.

A different method can be seen in wind towers. At street level, airflow was interrupted by walls and other obstacles. The tower inlet stood above neighbouring rooftops, where moving air was stronger and steadier. Openings at the top collected this air and directed it down a vertical shaft. In simple towers, it entered the rooms directly. In other designs, the air passed across a pool or a damp surface before entering the building, so evaporation reduced its temperature further.
Finally, we need to consider materials and internal design. Thick walls of stone or earth absorbed heat during the day. This did not mean heat never passed through them. Instead, the inner surface remained cool for a long period. More precisely, the thermal mass produced a long delay before the highest outdoor temperature affected the interior. By then, the outside air might already be cooling.
This explains how the wall helped control temperature, but some earthen materials offered another benefit. They absorbed water vapour when the air was damp and released it in drier conditions, helping to control indoor humidity.
Temperature and moisture were not the only concerns. Domestic design also had to balance the movement of air with the occupants’ social needs. Privacy was especially important where windows faced a public street. Lattice screens broke up the view from outside, so passers-by could not see clearly into the rooms. Yet their many small openings did not seal the window, and air could still pass through.
At the top of the house, builders deliberately separated the ceiling from what lay above. They left a ventilated space above the ceiling and immediately beneath the outer roof. Air moving through this space carried away accumulated heat and prevented much of it from reaching the rooms below.
So these buildings combined shade, controlled airflow, evaporation and carefully chosen materials. Their effectiveness came from treating the building and its surroundings as one connected system.
