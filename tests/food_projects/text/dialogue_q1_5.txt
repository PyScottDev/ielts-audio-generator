HANNAH: Let’s begin our presentation with our own projects, then discuss the wider developments.
BEN: Agreed. Why did you choose oat pulp? Because people overlook its nutritional value?
HANNAH: I discovered that later. I’d also seen recipes using it in bread, but no proper trials. What started it was the café near my flat. It produces roughly the same amount of pulp every day and offered to let me collect it regularly, so I knew I’d have enough for repeated tests.
BEN: That makes sense. What was the hardest part? I’d have thought getting all the water out would take ages.
HANNAH: So did I. But pressing it between metal trays worked immediately, and the taste was mild. The problem was shaping it. Unless every piece was nearly identical, the thin ones burnt before the thicker ones cooked.
BEN: I remember that irregular first batch.
HANNAH: Exactly. I spent weeks trying different cutters before I could produce pieces of a uniform size.
BEN: The basic material costs nothing, although drying it uses energy.
HANNAH: And production isn’t simple; the temperature needs controlling at several stages.
BEN: The real strength is the story cafés could tell: waste from their customers’ oat drinks becomes a snack they can buy. That gives cafés a strong way of promoting it.
HANNAH: Yes, much stronger than simply calling it a cheap snack. Now, how did the edible coating for fruit go?
BEN: Not too badly at first. I was worried it might leave the strawberries looking dull, but once it dried you could hardly see it.
HANNAH: Though keeping all those samples in the department fridge can’t have been easy.
BEN: There was enough space. The real issue was the fruit. One delivery contained small, firm strawberries; the next had softer, riper ones. They absorbed the coating differently, so I couldn’t compare the results reliably. That’s why I stopped.
HANNAH: Could you try a different ingredient in the coating?
BEN: Possibly, but I don’t yet know whether people would accept the texture of the version I’ve already made.
HANNAH: Then run a tasting session. If consumers compare coated and uncoated pieces, you’ll know whether it’s worth developing.
BEN: Good idea. I’d considered approaching a fruit producer, but that should come after I’ve got reactions from potential customers.
